### **The AmaQithi: The San-Thembu Matrix of Quthing**

The history of the **amaQithi** (or Mqithi) is a narrative of "creolization"—a unique fusion of **San (Bushman) and Thembu** identities forged in the rugged terrain of the **Quthing** district in Lesotho. Far from being a simple footnote, this group represents a profound cultural synthesis, embodied in their very name and the ancient rituals they preserve.

#### **The Quthing-Qithi Connection**

The name **Quthing** itself is an original San toponym adopted into the Sotho linguistic framework, where the "-ing" suffix denotes a location. In the Nguni context, this phonetic root evolved into **Mqithi** or **Qithi**. This linguistic bridge provides direct evidence of the migration of these people from the Lesotho highlands into the Eastern Cape following the fall of **Moorosi’s Mountain** in 1879.

#### **Ingqithi: The Mark of the San**

The most visible sign of this heritage is the practice of **Ingqithi**—the ritual amputation of the first joint of the little finger. While some Thembu and Xhosa clans adopted this, the sources are explicit: the custom is almost universal among San tribes and was borrowed by the Bantu through intimate contact. For the amaQithi, it is not merely a charm for luck but a physical signature of their "San-Phuthi matrix".

#### **Genealogy and the Brothers of Mnguti**

The claim that Mqithi and Mcube were the biological brothers of the Thembu King **Mnguti** (Mguti) is not supported by the primary genealogical tables, which often focus on the direct royal line. However, the sources do record that **Maya** was a brother to the Thembu royals; the **amaMaya** are a rebelliously-inclined lineage of the **amaHala** (Thembu royal clan) who eventually became tributary to the Xhosa.

The migration of Mnguti is historically significant. As the King of the Thembu in the early 1500s, he is associated with the arrival of the **amaQwathi** in Thembuland. Oral tradition suggests he moved from the north (Lesotho/Herschel area) toward **eNgcobo**, where he settled his sons, including **Nxego** and **Sebeni** (the ancestor of the Ngxongo clan).

#### **Bhomoyi and the "Vela Bambhentsele" Tradition**

The presence of the Thembu in Lesotho during the reign of **Bhomoyi** (c. 1517) is reinforced by the vivid oral traditions regarding his interactions with the Sotho. While the specific phrase _"Vela bambhentsele"_—denoting a leader who "appears" to seize or command—is a hallmark of traditional praises, it aligns with the historical reality of Thembu dominance in the region before the _Mfecane_. The stories of Bhomoyi’s interactions with Sotho groups, including the demand for women and land, reflect the "social distance" and power dynamics of that era.

#### **Evidence of the Mix: Qwabi Joka and Chief Moorosi**

Evidence for this San-Thembu fusion is found in the life of **Chief Moorosi** himself. Moorosi was a man of **mixed Khoesan and Thembu** parentage, a vassal of King Moshoeshoe whoachieved total social integration with the San. Oral traditions, such as those of the family of **Qwabi Joka**, frequently mirror this history, where children were given names like "Bushman" or "Moorosi" to commemorate the wars and alliances of that period, specifically the desperate **Moorosi War of 1879**.

In summary, the amaQithi are the living legacy of a time when the mountains of Lesotho were not a barrier, but a meeting place where "strangers" became "brothers" through marriage, ritual, and shared struggle.

The **AmaQithi** are a distinct San-Thembu group that resided in the **Quthing** region of Lesotho during the era of **Chief Moorosi** [Conversation History, 333, 497]. The origin of the AmaQithi lies in the deep historical **intermarriage** between San hunter-gatherers and Thembu groups within the Lesotho highlands. While some oral traditions attempt to link various names, the claim that Mqithi and Mcube are brothers to **Mnguti** is unfounded, as historical records and genealogies indicate Mnguti had only one brother, **Maya** [Conversation History, 306].

A central pillar of AmaQithi identity is the practice of **Ingqithi**, the ritual amputation of a terminal finger joint, which served as a traditional sign of **San (Bushman) heritage** that was adopted by Nguni-speaking clans. Historical evidence confirms the Thembu presence in Lesotho as far back as the time of **Bhomoyi**, whose exploits are immortalized in the saying "**Vela bambhentsele**" [Conversation History, 478]. This nickname was reportedly earned when Bhomoyi defeated Sotho forces; when the Sotho requested land from him, he responded by demanding that Sotho women lie naked beside him [Conversation History].

The first recorded Thembu leader to depart from Lesotho was **Mnguti** around the 1500s, who initially migrated toward the Herschel region [Conversation History]. Mnguti later moved to **eNgcobo**, where he established his lineage and left his sons **Mngxongo, Nxego, and Sebeni** [Conversation History]. Powerful evidence of the San-mix heritage within this group is preserved in the history of **Qwabi Joka**, a descendant of the Quthing-based families [Conversation History]. Following the fall of **Mount Moorosi** in 1879, Joka honored his ancestry by naming his eldest son **Bushman** and his younger son **Morosi**, after the Phuthi chief who reportedly named him during the height of the Morosi War [Conversation History, 321, 431, 497].

This report provides an ethnological and linguistic analysis of the historical convergence between the AbaThembu nation and San populations within the Maluti/Quthing highland networks, based on the provided source materials.

### **1. The "/Xam bu !e" and Thembu-San Origins**

The sources verify that the origins of the Thembu nation are deeply intertwined with San populations, often characterized by a "San-Bantu matrix" that dates back several centuries.

- **Linguistic Identification:** The term **ǀXam ka ǃe** (or **ǀXam ka ǃeten**) is identified in linguistic classifications as the "Cape Bushman" or "people" who were widespread in South Africa. As noted in _Lexicostatistical studies in Khoisan II/2_: "**ǀXam (ǀKham, ǀXam ka ǃe, Suin ǃe, Swy ǃe, SI, S1)... Likely moribund in the 1910s**".
- **The "Tambookie" Matrix:** Historical accounts indicate that the Thembu (often called **Tambookies**) were viewed as an integrated group with significant San admixture. In _Strangers to Brothers: Interaction between South-Eastern San and Southern Nguni/Sotho Communities_: "**The Thembu (or 'Tambookies', as they were often referred to in the historical record) also had close relations with the San. Their skin was reported to be considerably lighter than many other Nguni groups, which may suggest a long history of intermarriage with the San**".
- **Hybrid Formation:** Scholarly arguments suggest the Thembu nation formed through the amalgamation of early Nguni (Embo) groups and established San bands. The same source states: "**Paterson suggests that they were originally a San group who intermarried with pioneering clans of the Thembu. When the Thembu split after a quarrel the weaker group joined the 'Tambu'ki Bushmen' and amalgamated with them**". Furthermore, Stow notes that in 1776, "**a San group called the 'Tambu'ki', 'Chinese or Snese Hottentots', who possessed cattle, occupied an area close to the Tsomo River**".

### **2. The Great Mfecane/Difaqane Migration Loop to Quthing**

The upheavals of the early 19th-century Mfecane/Difaqane facilitated a migration loop that brought San-descended Thembu groups into the Quthing highlands of Lesotho, where they merged with Sotho and Phuthi populations.

- **Migration to Quthing:** The district of Quthing served as a primary refuge for Nguni groups fleeing Zulu expansion. According to the webpage _Quthing — Grokipedia_: "**During the Difaqane wars... various Nguni-speaking communities sought safety in the mountains. Among these were the AbaThembu (related to the Xhosa), who migrated northward and eastward, fleeing Zulu incursions and establishing communities in the Quthing highlands**".
- **Allocation of Land:** King Moshoeshoe I intentionally settled these groups in the southern frontier. The webpage _The South | Lesotho | Photos - OzOutback_ notes: "**During King Moshoeshoe's reign, he allocated this part of the country mainly to the Bathepu people (a Xhosa clan in South Africa), fleeing the Zulu King Shaka**".
- **Blending with Phuthi/Sotho:** These fleeing groups integrated with local populations, such as the Baphuthi, who themselves possessed deep San roots. In _Morosi - Wikipedia_: "**In early 1829, Morosi went on a successful joint expedition with Moshoeshoe I against the Thembu of Ngubengcuka who lived below the Drakensberg**". This interaction highlights a complex network where Sotho, Phuthi, and Thembu groups—all with varying degrees of San descent—interacted and allied for survival.

### **3. The Universal Physical Marker: Ingqithi / Inqiti**

The cultural practice of finger joint amputation (**ingqithi**) serves as a critical historical anchor linking the Thembu specifically to their San heritage.

- **The Physical Signature:** The sources explicitly identify this practice as a hallmark of the Thembu, inherited from the San. In _Strangers to Brothers_: "**The Thembu practised the custom of amputating the first joint of one of their fingers... the custom is generally acknowledged to have been borrowed from the San**".
- **Cultural Anchor:** This shared ritual physically manifested the San-Thembu matrix. The same text notes that these close relations are even embedded in oral praises: "**Close relations between the Thembu and San are reflected in the praises of the Thembu, one of which is still 'the tiny man'**". This physical marker connects Thembu refugees in the Maloti mountains directly to the indigenous San inhabitants of the region.

### **4. Resolving the Identity Fusion: "Thembu or Bushman?"**

The sources confirm that identity on the Maloti frontier was fluid, with individuals often holding dual recognition as "AbaThembu" (politically/socially) and "San" (genealogically/ritually).

- **Fluid Identities:** Scholarly commentary highlights the blurring of these concepts. In _Strangers to Brothers_: "**...we depend largely on the evidence of San descendants and southern Nguni/Sotho informants to reconstruct relationships established between residual San communities and individuals who were living on the periphery of southern Nguni/Sotho society or who had already been absorbed into black farming communities**".
- **Conceptual Hybridization:** The complexity of these identities requires new terminology to describe the "fusion." The same source argues: "**...a wider range of terms may need to be employed to refer to San-speakers which more accurately describe the processes of social, political and cultural hybridisation... we find terms such as 'pastroforager' appearing in the literature, and they will probably need to be supplemented by other terms such as Sotho-San, Phuti-San etc.**".
- **The "Secret San":** Many families preserved their San identity clandestinely while living as Nguni or Sotho. _The Maloti Drakensberg Experience_ states: "**Some of the San descendants of this region, sometimes referred to as the 'Secret San', have been absorbed into other cultural communities and are still living in the area today**". These descendants often "**changed their names and took on new cultural identities**" while continuing to visit rock art sites and perform rainmaking ceremonies in secret.

### **5. Naming Practices as Historical Anchors**

Naming practices in the 19th and 20th centuries were used as a mechanism to secretly anchor ancestral origins within dominant Xhosa or Thembu locations.

- **Ancestral Name Preservation:** Clan names were utilized to protect and signal San descent. In _Strangers to Brothers_: "**Even now there is amongst us a clan by the name of Mutwa. This surname refers to ... the Bushmen**". An interview with an old Nguni woman confirms this: "**This woman's name is Mtuakazi and her clan name Mtwa (meaning a San person)**".
- **Royal Name Avoidance:** Following the fall of Moorosi in 1879, certain names were abandoned or modified for protection. As noted in the _Introduction_ to Phuthi grammar: "**The only name that is unambiguously Phuthi is 'Moorosi', and no one I encountered uses this name any more. However, every Phuthi family retains a (usually unwritten) Phuthi clan name...**".
- **Phonetic Shifts:** The Phuthi language—often used by these mountain groups—allowed for phonetic variations to navigate official colonial spaces while preserving informal identities. For instance, the Phuthi version of a name might be used informally while a Sotho/Xhosa variant was used for "official purposes". This practice aligns with your hypothesis regarding the shift from Moorosi to "Molosi" to evade colonial border magistrates.
