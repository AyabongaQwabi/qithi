**Qing** was a young San man from the Maluti mountains of Lesotho who, in 1873, served as a guide and informant for the British colonial administrator **Joseph Millerd Orpen**. His testimony is regarded as a "Rosetta Stone" for rock art research because he provided the first direct oral traditions explaining the complex imagery found in San shelters.

### **Qing’s Background and the Orpen Interview**

Qing was the son of a San chief (believed by scholars to be **Chief Soai**) whose tribe had been largely exterminated by Sotho forces around 1871–1872. After the destruction of his band, Qing and his two wives sought refuge with **Nqasha**, a son of the Phuthi chief **Moorosi**. Because he lived among the Phuthi, Qing spoke **Seputhi**, a hybrid dialect of Xhosa and Sesotho [Du Plessis in 134, 540]. In 1873, while Orpen was tracking the chief Langalibalele, Qing guided him to several rock shelters, including **Melikane** and **Sehonghong (Mangolong)**, where Orpen copied the paintings while Qing explained their meaning.

### **Qweqwete and Abakhwetha**

A central part of Qing’s testimony involved identifying the figures in the paintings. In Orpen’s original manuscript, Qing referred to different classes of figures using specific names, most notably **Qweqwete** (also spelled _Quequete_ or _Gweqwete_ in the drafts).

- **Linguistic Link:** Modern researchers have identified **Qweqwete** as a San or Seputhi version of the Xhosa word **abakhwetha** (or _abakwetha_).
- **Definition of Abakhwetha:** In Xhosa culture, **abakhwetha** refers to initiates, specifically young men undergoing the rite of circumcision or student diviners undergoing training.
- **Depiction in Art:** Qing used the term "Qweqwete" to describe the "tailed men" depicted in the **upper Mangolong** painting. These "tails" are now believed to represent the woven grass costumes (the _liphiri_ or "wolves") worn by Sotho initiates during their seclusion. Furthermore, one of these figures has spots on its body, mirroring the white clay patterns traditionally painted on the bodies of Xhosa _abakhwetha_.

### **Interpretations of the Paintings**

Qing’s comments on the paintings at Melikane and upper Mangolong suggest a deep **cultural hybridization** between San and Bantu-speaking (Nguni/Sotho) traditions:

- **The Melikane Figures:** These figures are depicted in a **stooped posture** holding two sticks. This posture is identical to that adopted by **Xhosa and Zulu diviners** (_amagqirha_) and male initiates during specific rituals. The Sotho word for "one who bends forward" (_kgoko_) is the same as the word for a "great doctor or teacher," further linking the posture to ritual instruction.
- **Animal-Head Masks:** Qing identified these figures as having rhebok heads. While some scholars see this as a metaphor for a shaman's transformation, others note that Phuthi and Sotho ritual teachers (**basuoe**) were known to wear actual masks made from animal heads during initiation ceremonies.
- **"River People" Metaphor:** Qing remarked that these men had "died and now lived in rivers" and were "spoilt... by the dances". In San thought, "death" is a metaphor for the trance state. However, the concept of "river people" (_abantu bomlambo_) and "taming snakes" also features prominently in Nguni diviner training, where initiates are believed to travel underwater to meet ancestral spirits and capture water snakes for medicine.

In summary, **Qing’s** explanations of the paintings reveal that the **Qweqwete** (initiates/abakhwetha) were not just a San tradition, but a reflection of how San and Phuthi people shared a complex ritual life involving **circumcision, diviner initiation, and trance**.
