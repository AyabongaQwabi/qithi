In the history of **Lesotho** (formerly Basutoland) and its surrounding highlands, the **abaThembu** appear as early pioneers, refugee migrants, and key participants in a unique cultural synthesis with the San. Their presence is particularly significant in the **Quthing district** and the **Mbashe-to-Orange River corridors**.

### **Early Settlement and the San-Thembu Matrix**

The abaThembu are identified as one of the first Bantu-speaking nations to migrate south from central Africa, living for long periods in the highveld and coastal regions before establishing Thembuland proper.

- **The Quthing Synthesis:** The Quthing district served as a "creolized" zone where pioneering Thembu clans achieved total social and linguistic integration with the San. This synthesis was most prominent under **Chief Moorosi**, a leader of mixed **Khoesan and Thembu parentage** who ruled the Phuti (a group related to the Thembu).
- **Toponymic Links:** The name **Quthing** is an original San toponym adopted into the Sotho linguistic framework (the "-ing" suffix being a locative). In the Nguni context, this root evolved into **Mqithi** or **Qithi**, providing a linguistic map for the migration of these lineages from Lesotho into the Eastern Cape.
- **The "Tambu'ki Bushmen":** Historical records from the late 18th century describe a group called the "Tambu'ki" or "Snese Hottentots" living near the Orange River. These were likely a mixture of San and pioneering Thembu clans who intermarried and amalgamated.

### **Significant Lineages in Lesotho**

- **The amaVundle:** This major Thembu-related lineage in Lesotho originated from a split in the **Bafokeng** tribe at Ntsuanatsatsi. When a Bafokeng chief took a San woman as his principal wife, his Sotho subjects refused to be ruled by her San-descended son. This group migrated to Thembuland, adopted the Thembu language and culture, and eventually resettled in southern Basutoland in the **Mjanyane Valley**.
- **Chief Tjale:** In the early 20th century, **Tjale** was identified as the chief of the Tembus of the Motjanyane in Quthing. He confirmed the oral traditions of the Fokeng-San-Thembu migration.
- **The amaXesibe:** Though identifying as Thembu by allegiance, the amaXesibe were driven north into Basutoland by internal conflicts, though they were later compelled to leave by the Basotho.

### **19th-Century Migrations and Conflict**

The relationship between the abaThembu and the Basotho was defined by both shared refuge and territorial contestation.

- **The amaQwathi Migration:** The **amaQwathi** (Thembu by allegiance) once sought to settle forcibly in Basutoland after being driven north from Pondoland. They remained there briefly but were eventually expelled by the Basotho and returned to Thembuland as suppliants under King **Ngubencuka**.
- **Conflict with King Moshoeshoe:** The Basotho King **Moshoeshoe** once launched a major cattle raid into Thembuland, reaching as far as **Nywara** (Ngubencuka's Great Place). However, the Sotho forces were eventually **vanquished and expelled** by a joint force of amaHala and amaQwathi.
- **The Moorosi War (1879):** The fall of Moorosi's mountain stronghold marked a turning point, leading surviving Phuthi and San families to flee Lesotho and settle in the Eastern Cape borderlands, forming the **Qithi (Mqithi)** lineages found there today.

### **Customs and Identification**

The close association between these groups in Lesotho is attested to by shared cultural markers. The **Thembu custom of Ingqithi** (ritual amputation of the first joint of the little finger) is explicitly acknowledged in the sources as a practice **borrowed from the San**. Furthermore, Thembu praise songs often include references to "the tiny man," reflecting their long history of intermarriage and social symbiosis with the San of the Lesotho highlands.
