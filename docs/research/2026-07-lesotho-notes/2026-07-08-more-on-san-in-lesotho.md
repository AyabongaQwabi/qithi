The history of the **San (Baroa)** in the **Quthing** district of Lesotho is inextricably linked to the **Baphuthi** people and their leader, **Chief Moorosi**. The sources characterize this relationship as a deep, symbiotic convergence of cultures through intermarriage, military alliance, and shared environmental knowledge.

### **1. The San Presence in Quthing**

The Quthing district, located in southern Lesotho, has been inhabited by San hunter-gatherers for thousands of years.

- **Toponymy and Language:** The name "**Quthing**" itself reflects this heritage, as the "Q" sound is a **palatal click integrated from San languages** into Sesotho during prolonged historical interaction. In the Phuthi language, the area is called **Qûthînî**.
- **Rock Art:** The rugged landscape is marked by extensive San rock art, with notable sites near **Mount Moorosi** and the **Tsatsane Valley**. These paintings depict daily life, spiritual beliefs, and rituals such as the **trance dance**.
- **Interaction with Bantu Groups:** The Baphuthi were among the first Bantu-speaking groups to arrive in the region, where they came into immediate contact and **intermarried with the autochthonous San communities**.

### **2. Chief Moorosi and His San Heritage**

Moorosi (died 1879), the paramount chief of the Baphuthi, is described as a pivotal figure who bridged the gap between Bantu and San societies.

- **Ancestry and Appearance:** Moorosi was widely reported to be of **mixed San descent on his mother’s side**. His physical appearance—described as small in stature with "yellow skin"—was noted by colonial officials as evidence of this heritage.
- **San Wives and Family:** Moorosi **married at least two San wives** and had several children with them. One of his wives was recorded as having the San name **Quane**.
- **Cultural Exchange:** His son, **Mapote**, famously recalled that his **San half-brothers taught him the art of rock painting**, illustrating the direct transmission of San cultural practices into the Phuthi royal line.

### **3. The Baphuthi-San Alliance**

Under Moorosi’s leadership, the Baphuthi became known as the **"last protectors" of the San** in the Maloti-Drakensberg region.

- **Equality and Protection:** San living under Moorosi’s authority were considered **equal to the Baphuthi** under the laws against killing. Anyone harming a San person under his protection incurred Moorosi's "wrath".
- **Economic Cooperation:** The groups were highly integrated; they **hunted eland together**, traded horses, and herded livestock. By 1869, some San lived in Phuthi villages in huts, possessing their own cattle and cultivating crops.
- **Military Partnership:** San archers frequently fought alongside the Baphuthi in raids against European and other Bantu farmers.

### **4. Moorosi’s War (1879) and the Siege**

The ultimate expression of this alliance occurred during Moorosi's rebellion against Cape Colonial rule.

- **The Conflict:** The war was triggered by colonial attempts to enforce hut taxes and disarmament.
- **San in Combat:** San armed with bows and arrows fought alongside the Phuthi, helping them inflict significant losses on colonial troops.
- **The Password:** The deep significance of the San to the Phuthi is evidenced by the fact that the **password** for Phuthi wanting access to Moorosi's mountain fortress during the eight-month siege was "**Moroa**," the Sotho term for "**Bushman**".
- **The Aftermath:** When the fortress fell on November 20, 1879, Moorosi was killed and beheaded. His subjects, including his San allies, were **massacred or dispersed**, fleeing into the Eastern Cape to evade capture.

### **5. Linguistic and Archival Legacy: Qing**

Much of what is known about the Maloti San comes from **Qing**, a San man who was a son of the leader Soai.

- **Linguistic Hybridity:** Qing was a multilingual guide who spoke **Phuthi**, which Orpen described as a "hybrid dialect" between Xhosa and Sesotho.
- **San-Phuthi Connection:** After his own band was scattered, Qing sought refuge with **Moorosi’s son, Nqasha**, highlighting that the Phuthi royal family remained a sanctuary for the San even as their traditional way of life collapsed.
- **Rock Art Interpretation:** Qing’s commentary on rock art at Melikane and Sehonghong provided the "insider’s knowledge" that underpins modern southern African rock art research.
