The history of the **San (Baroa)** in the **Quthing** region of Lesotho is characterized by a profound and symbiotic alliance with **Chief Moorosi** and the **BaPhuthi** people. This relationship, which included extensive intermarriage, economic trade, and military cooperation, was so deep that the Phuthi chiefdom functioned as a "federation" that fully integrated San culture and individuals.

### **The San-Phuthi Alliance and Chief Moorosi**

Chief Moorosi’s leadership was defined by his role as the "last protector of the San" in the Maloti-Drakensberg region.

- **Mixed Heritage:** Moorosi himself was reported to be of **San descent** on his mother’s side. Major David Hook, who met the chief, described him as a small man with "yellow skin," reflecting this heritage.
- **Familial Ties:** The alliance was formalized when Moorosi’s father, **Mokuoane**, married the sister of the **San chief Quu**. Under Quu’s influence, the San and Phuthi lived together in mountain caves, with the San providing game and survival expertise during the disruptions of the **Difaqane**.
- **Cultural Signs:** The **Ingqithi** ritual (the amputation of a finger joint) was a cultural marker of San heritage adopted by several clans in the region, including the **AmaQithi**, who are of San-Thembu descent and originated in the **emaQithini (Quthing)** area [Conversation History].

### **Social and Economic Integration**

By the mid-19th century, the San in Quthing were significantly integrated into Phuthi society:

- **Settlement and Agriculture:** Statements from 1869 indicate that many San lived in huts within Phuthi villages, where they possessed **cattle and sheep** and cultivated crops.
- **Seasonal Mobility:** They often moved between Phuthi villages and the mountains, returning to the hills at "picking" (ploughing) time to hunt.
- **Trade:** Phuthi leaders traded **tobacco and dagga** to the San in exchange for ostrich feathers and animal skins. The San used dagga acquired through this trade to help attain the **trance states** necessary for their ritual dances.

### **Rock Art and Intellectual Exchange**

Quthing and the surrounding valleys (such as the Sebapala) remain rich in San rock art, which served as a medium for cultural exchange:

- **Collaborative Painting:** Moorosi’s son, **Mapote**, lived and **painted rock art with the San** in his youth.
- **Phuthi Artists:** A Phuthi artist named **Masitise** executed finely detailed paintings at the British Residency in Quthing, including scenes depicting battles between rival San leaders.
- **Qing’s Testimony:** **Qing**, believed to be the son of the San leader **Soai**, sought refuge with Moorosi’s son **Nqasha** after his father's band was destroyed in 1872. Qing’s explanations of rock art at Sehonghong and Melikane are critical for modern understanding of San cosmology.

### **Military Cooperation and the Siege of Mount Moorosi**

The bond between the San and Phuthi reached its peak during the **1879 rebellion** against the British Colonial government:

- **The "Moroa" Password:** During the eight-month siege of the cliff-fringed fortress at **Mount Moorosi**, the high-security password used by defenders was **"Moroa"** (meaning **"Bushman"**).
- **Joint Defense:** San warriors "armed with bows and arrows" fought alongside the Phuthi to repulse Colonial assaults.
- **The Fall of the Fortress:** When Mount Moorosi was finally stormed in November 1879, Chief Moorosi was killed, and many of his followers and San allies were scattered. Following the war, San-descended families, such as the **Qwabi and Joka lines**, migrated from the Quthing region to the **Queenstown (Komani)** and **Lady Frere** areas of the Eastern Cape [Conversation History].
