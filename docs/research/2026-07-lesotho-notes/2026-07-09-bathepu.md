The following document provides an academically rigorous overview of the AbaThembu (Bathepu) and San (Baroa) historical convergence in the Quthing district of Lesotho, incorporating archival data on the San-Bantu matrix, the ritual of ingqithi, and the 1879 defense of Chief Moorosi.
Research Report: The Bathepu-San Matrix and the Highland Frontier

1. Ethnogenesis and the "Tambookie" Hybridity
   The core identity of the AbaThembu nation in the Maloti-Drakensberg frontier is defined by a historical amalgamation of early Nguni (Embo) groups and autochthonous San bands. This fusion created a distinct demographic referred to in early colonial records as "Tambookies." These groups were often described as possessing "lighter skin," a physical trait attributed to centuries of intermarriage with San populations 1, 2. Historical evidence suggests some Thembu sections were originally San groups who integrated into pioneering Nguni clans following internal tribal schisms 1.
2. The Mfecane and the Quthing Sanctuary
   During the 19th-century Difaqane (Mfecane), the expansion of the Zulu Kingdom under King Shaka forced numerous communities to seek refuge in the mountains. The AbaThembu (Bathepu) migrated northward into the Lesotho highlands 3, 4. King Moshoeshoe I strategically settled these refugees in the southern frontier, specifically allocating the Quthing district to the Bathepu to serve as a buffer zone 3, 5. This region already possessed deep San roots, allowing the newcomers to integrate further into the existing Phuthi-San environmental and social networks 4, 6.
3. Cultural Anchors: Ingqithi and Shuffling Dances
   The deep bond between the Thembu and San is preserved through specific ritual signatures. The practice of ingqithi—the amputation of the first joint of the little finger—is a universal physical marker borrowed directly from Southern San customs 1. Additionally, Thembu traditional dances involve "shuffling" movements distinct from other Nguni groups, which researchers identify as being derived from San trance rituals 1. In traditional Thembu society, San companions were historically granted precedence in hunting parties, reflecting their status as the original "owners of the land" 1.
4. The Alliance of 1879: Mount Moorosi
   During the eight-month siege of Mount Moorosi in 1879, the alliances reflected the deep integration of the southern frontier. Chief Moorosi, himself of mixed San descent, was supported by San archers and local Bathepu villagers 4, 6. While official Basotho leadership (Letsie I) supported the Cape Colony, local Bathepu risking their lives under cover of darkness were essential for replenishing the food supplies of the Phuthi and San warriors atop the mountain 4. The centrality of this alliance was encoded in the fortress security; the password for access was "Moroa," the Sotho term for "Bushman" 4.
5. Modern Legal Recognition
   The historical presence of the Bathepu as a linguistic minority has culminated in recent constitutional shifts. In August 2025, through the Tenth Amendment to the Constitution of Lesotho, isiXhosa (the language of the Bathepu) and siPhuthi were officially recognized as national languages 7. This move aimed to address the long-standing marginalization of these communities in the Quthing and Qacha’s Nek districts 7.
